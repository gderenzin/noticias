Hackers help themselves to 1Password's source code and customer data

Fuente: The Register - Security Publicado: 26/11/2026 · 11:02 UTC
Compartir:

1Pass­word is reas­su­ring cus­to­mers that their vaults re­main se­cu­re after an at­tac­ker brea­ched its sys­tems and stole ac­count in­for­ma­tion and pri­va­te sour­ce code. The Ca­na­dian se­cu­rity biz dis­clo­sed this week that an at­tac­ker had com­pro­mi­sed an em­plo­yee's cor­po­ra­te GitHub ac­count, made off with pri­va­te sour­ce code, and de­plo­yed ma­li­cious code to its bac­kend ser­vi­ces to siphon off cus­to­mer in­for­ma­tion. 1Pass­word, which began in­ves­ti­ga­ting the in­tru­sion on No­vem­ber 19, hasn't said how many cus­to­mers were af­fec­ted, but con­fir­med that the sto­len data in­clu­des names and email ad­dres­ses used to sign in to the ser­vi­ce, ac­count iden­ti­fiers, ti­mes­tamps sho­wing when users crea­ted and last ac­ces­sed their ac­counts, and va­rious pie­ces of non-se­cret ac­count me­ta­da­ta. The com­pany in­sists the in­for­ma­tion that cus­to­mers ac­tually pay it to pro­tect was be­yond the in­tru­der's reach. "At no time were cus­to­mer vault con­tents ac­ces­sed by, or avai­la­ble to, the at­tac­ker," 1Pass­word said. Ar­guably more trou­bling is the re­ve­la­tion that the in­tru­der gai­ned ac­cess to 1Pass­word's con­ti­nuous in­te­gra­tion and con­ti­nuous de­li­very (CI/CD) in­fras­truc­tu­re, which hou­ses cre­den­tials used to keep its pro­ducts run­ning. The com­pany says it has ro­ta­ted or re­vo­ked po­ten­tially ex­po­sed cre­den­tials and that neither its apps nor its brow­ser ex­ten­sions were af­fec­ted. 1Pass­word hasn't ex­plai­ned how ac­cess to an em­plo­yee's GitHub ac­count enabled the at­tac­ker to de­ploy ma­li­cious code to its bac­kend ser­vi­ces, nor has it de­tai­led the ex­tent of the at­tac­ker's ac­cess to its soft­wa­re build and dis­tri­bu­tion in­fras­truc­tu­re. The com­pany did not res­pond to The Re­gis­ter's ques­tions. The mis­creant also got their hands on to­kens em­bed­ded in emails sent to some cus­to­mers as part of ac­count in­vi­ta­tions, pro­vi­sio­ning, and re­co­very pro­ces­ses. These to­kens aren't enough to ac­cess an ac­count on their own, ac­cor­ding to 1Pass­word, as users must also authen­ti­ca­te via sin­gle sign-on or enter a ve­ri­fi­ca­tion code sent to their re­gis­te­red email ad­dress. The com­pany says it spe­ci­fi­cally no­ti­fied the small num­ber of users with af­fec­ted pro­vi­sio­ning or re­co­very pro­ces­ses un­der­way. It found no in­ter­nal evi­den­ce of ac­count ta­keo­vers using the ex­po­sed pro­vi­sio­ning to­kens. Still, the breach has promp­ted the pass­word ma­na­ge­ment biz to beef up those ve­ri­fi­ca­tion codes, re­pla­cing the six-digit nu­me­ric va­riety with ten-cha­rac­ter alpha­nu­me­ric codes to make brute-for­cing them con­si­de­rably more dif­fi­cult. 1Pass­word has boo­ted the in­tru­der out of its GitHub en­vi­ron­ment, re­vo­ked the sto­len token, and ro­lled back the af­fec­ted bac­kend ser­vi­ces to clean ver­sions of the code. It's since sho­red up se­cu­rity around its de­ve­lop­ment in­fras­truc­tu­re and em­plo­yees' de­vi­ces, and brought in outsi­de se­cu­rity spe­cia­lists to re­view its han­di­work. The in­ci­dent re­calls the first stage of Last­Pass's 2022 breach, when an at­tac­ker com­pro­mi­sed its de­ve­lop­ment en­vi­ron­ment and stole sour­ce code. In­for­ma­tion ob­tai­ned in that in­tru­sion hel­ped enable a se­cond at­tack that ex­po­sed cus­to­mer vault bac­kups, in­clu­ding encry­pted pass­words. The UK In­for­ma­tion Com­mis­sion's Of­fi­ce slap­ped Last­Pass with a £1.2 mi­llion ($1.6 mi­llion) fine last De­cem­ber over the in­ci­dent, which com­pro­mi­sed the per­so­nal in­for­ma­tion of up to 1.6 mi­llion UK users. Among the re­gu­la­tor's gri­pes was that Last­Pass had fa­llen short of the higher stan­dard of se­cu­rity ex­pec­ted of a com­pany en­trus­ted with peo­ple's pass­words. Cus­to­mers aren't being asked to chan­ge their ac­count pass­words or Se­cret Keys, neither of which 1Pass­word sto­res, although the com­pany is war­ning users to be on the loo­kout for phishing at­tem­pts. A list of email ad­dres­ses be­lon­ging to peo­ple known to use a par­ti­cu­lar pass­word ma­na­ger is a handy thing for a phisher to have, es­pe­cially when said pass­word ma­na­ger has just given them a plau­si­ble pre­text for get­ting in touch. ®…
